1. WHAT THE PREVIOUS PAPER DID

Reference: Sven Goessens (2024), "Predicting the Potential Growth of Football
Players in the EA Sports FC Games: A Comparative Study on Logistic Regression,
Random Forest and XGBoost," Tilburg University master's thesis. [1]

1.1 Research question and data

The paper asked whether attributes in the EA Sports FC video game could identify
players who would later become "high potential" IN THE GAME. It did not establish
that the game's scores are objective measurements of real-world match performance.
Its source was Stefano Leone's Kaggle collection, covering editions from 2015
through 2023. [1, pp. 2, 4–5, 11]

1.2 Cleaning and preparation

- The source included multiple updates for some players within an edition. The
  study retained the first observation for each player in each year.
- It removed editions 2015 and 2016 because fewer attributes were available.
- It excluded goalkeepers because their abilities differ from outfield players.
- It selected relevant columns and encoded categorical fields such as preferred
  foot and playable positions. The paper reports 190,231 rows and 49 features
  after its initial cleaning. Requiring a known three-year future reduced the
  analysis to 40,669 rows. [1, pp. 11–14]

1.3 The published target

The study calculated differences in player attribute totals over future one-,
two-, and three-year periods. Its final target was BINARY: a player counted as
"high potential" only if (a) the total attribute points increased by at least
89 within the next three years AND (b) the player's Overall rating reached at
least 76 at some point during 2017–2023. Otherwise the label was zero. The
paper reports that 6.44% of its final 40,669 observations had the positive
label. These figures are the study's definitions and sample counts, not results
measured in my current project. [1, pp. 13–14]

1.4 Models, evaluation and conclusions

The author compared Logistic Regression, Random Forest and XGBoost. The study
used stratified 10-fold cross-validation, SMOTE in the training folds to address
class imbalance, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning. It assessed
class-specific and macro precision, recall and F1, accuracy, Cohen's kappa and
confusion matrices. It also examined feature importance and whether splitting
players by position improved results. The author reported that Random Forest
identified the high-potential minority class comparatively well, while
position-based segmentation did not produce a major improvement. These are the
paper's findings; they are not claims about my proposed model. [1, pp. 2, 17–21,
35–36]

2. WHAT MY CURRENT LAB PROJECT DOES

The existing Football-Position-Classification repository loads my local
FIFA_Male_Players_2015_2023.csv (161,583 rows and 110 original columns in the
recorded run), selects 41 numeric predictors, and maps the first listed player
position to one of four groups. It trains Random Forest and XGBoost, keeping
players separated between training and test partitions. Its Flask endpoint
returns a position-group prediction; the Angular frontend displays that result
when run locally. GitHub Pages is a static frontend demonstration and does not
host a live Python inference server. None of this code currently forecasts a
future player's Overall rating or whether the player will appear next year. [2]

Consequently, my position classifier is a practical lab baseline on the same
football source, NOT a reproduction of the published paper's high-potential
target, data filtering, or reported numerical results.

3. PROPOSED NEW RESEARCH IDEA (FUTURE WORK)

Working question: Can a history-aware, two-stage model predict (1) whether an
EA Sports FC player has a rating in the next edition and, if so, (2) whether
that next-edition Overall rating rises substantially, stays similar, or falls
substantially?

The idea in one line: predict whether the player will have a rating in the
next FIFA/FC edition; then, conditional on a next-edition rating existing,
predict whether that rating will rise sharply, stay similar, or fall sharply.

3.1 Why a two-stage prediction is needed

For each player observed in edition t, look up the same player ID in edition
t+1. There are two logically different possible outcomes:

  A. No next-edition record is observed in this dataset: a next-edition
     Overall rating cannot be computed from the available records.
  B. A next-edition record exists: compute delta = Overall(t+1) - Overall(t)
     and classify the change as increase, stable, or decrease.

An absent record is NOT evidence that the player became worse, retired, or
left professional football. A dataset can omit players for several reasons.
The model should describe its first-stage result precisely as "record present
in the next edition" rather than "still playing football."

3.2 Planned data construction

1. Order the available edition snapshots by player_id and fifa_version. Define
   one documented snapshot per player per edition if the CSV includes updates.
2. For every usable edition t, construct features only from information
   available by that edition: current age, Overall, position and skill ratings,
   plus available prior-edition ratings and their changes. Use a missing-history
   indicator for a player seen for the first time rather than inventing a past.
3. Label Stage 1 as 1 if the player's ID appears in t+1 in the dataset, or 0
   if not. Exclude the final available edition as a starting year, because its
   next edition is not in this 2015–2023 file.
4. For Stage 1 positives, define delta in Overall points. Agree on the
   "substantial" threshold before evaluating on held-out editions. For
   example, with a threshold of 3 points: delta >= +3 is Increase, delta <= -3
   is Decrease, and -2 through +2 is Stable. Three points is a PROPOSED
   definition for investigation, not a measured finding or a claim of
   statistical significance. Check class balance and run sensitivity analyses
   with thresholds chosen using training data only.
5. Check duplicate IDs, missing values and release-to-release changes in
   feature definitions; document which editions and players remain eligible.

3.3 Planned modelling and comparisons

- Stage 1: train an availability classifier using information known at t.
- Stage 2: among records with observed t+1 ratings in the training data,
  train a three-class rating-change classifier. Candidate models include
  Random Forest and XGBoost, with simple baselines such as a majority class,
  current age/rating model, and last observed rating-change rule.
- Compare a history-aware two-stage system against (a) a single-snapshot
  Stage 2 model and (b) a direct outcome model whose classes include
  No Next-Edition Record, Increase, Stable, and Decrease. These comparisons
  help test whether the added stages and history actually add value.
- Build the final output from the two models: if Stage 1 predicts no observed
  next-edition record, return that outcome; otherwise report one of the three
  rating-change classes. Document error propagation from Stage 1 to Stage 2.

3.4 Planned evaluation and leakage controls

Train on earlier editions, tune on a later edition, and test on still later
editions for which the following edition is actually present. Report the
edition cutoffs explicitly; do not allow rows from the test future to create
features, thresholds, imputation values or labels used in training. Account
for repeated observations of the same player when estimating uncertainty;
report separate evaluations for previously seen players and new players if
both appear in the test data.

Assess Stage 1 with a confusion matrix, class-specific precision and recall,
F1, and area under ROC and precision–recall curves where appropriate. Assess
Stage 2 with macro F1, per-class precision and recall, balanced accuracy and a
confusion matrix. Also report end-to-end results for all eligible starting
records, including the No Next-Edition Record outcome, and compare model
performance by edition, age band and position. Any claimed improvement must
be demonstrated on held-out editions, with uncertainty estimates where
feasible. In-game Potential is itself an EA forecast-like rating; compare
results with and without it rather than treating it as an unquestioned
independent predictor.

4. WHAT IS DIFFERENT, AND WHAT HAS NOT YET BEEN PROVEN

The Goessens thesis predicts a three-year binary high-potential label and
requires known future records for its final sample. My proposed output instead
explicitly models next-edition record availability and then separates sharp
increase, stability and sharp decline in the next-edition Overall rating. This
is a different task definition and proposed evaluation workflow on the same
Kaggle source. The current GitHub position-classification model is separate
from both tasks. [1][2]

Do NOT describe one-year forecasting, use of Random Forest/XGBoost, historical
features, or time-aware evaluation alone as inventions: other football work
already forecasts one-year player-quality changes with those model families
and time-series information, using a different data source and quality
indicator. [3] I cannot confirm that the EXACT two-stage formulation has
never been published or implemented elsewhere. Before making a publication
novelty claim, complete a documented literature and repository search and
compare datasets, targets, eligibility rules, evaluation splits and code.

No Stage 1 or Stage 2 model, outcome threshold, comparative score, discovery,
or publishable improvement is claimed as completed here. The proposed
research contribution will depend on empirical results, reproducibility and
how it compares with the closest existing work.
